Build the right AI tool

Learn to turn a job you repeat into a small AI tool that someone else can use, and to prove it works before anyone relies on it.

Who it is forBusiness owners, employees and students who use a chatbot at work. Modules 1 to 4 need no code. Module 5 adds a track for people who build with a coding agent.
What you need
  • A chatbot you already use, such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Copilot
  • One job you do on your own every week that starts from a blank page
  • About three and a half hours, in one day or over five weeks, plus the capstone
How it runsEach module has a lecture deck, step-by-step lessons, a three-question check and an assignment. It ends with a capstone: your own tool, through all nine steps.

By the end of the course you can

  1. Say what a tool does in one sentence: one user, one input, one result.
  2. Check what already exists, and decide whether to stop, wrap it or build.
  3. Write a one-page plan and the first screen a user will see, before anything is built.
  4. Test your tool with a stranger, a fresh chat that has only your instructions, and read what it tripped on.
  5. Grow a tool one addition at a time, and know which level of the process fits your work.

Module 4 · 1 / 8

Instructor notes. This is where most AI work stops: it works for me, so it works. Today we find out whether it works for someone else.

Build the smallest version

  • Instructions someone can paste
  • The steps, the rules that never bend, the format of the result

Module 4 · 2 / 8

Instructor notes. Leave out everything under 'what is out'. When the AI finishes, ask it what it decided, what it assumed and what it did not check.

The fresh-chat test

Module 4 · 3 / 8

Instructor notes. A new chat, temporary or with memory off, gets only your instructions and one real input. It does the job and lists every place it had to guess. You do not help it. You read the list before the result.

Blocking or minor

Blocking

  • A different guess would change the result
  • Fix it

Minor

  • The result would be the same
  • Note it; fix it if cheap

Module 4 · 4 / 8

Instructor notes. Every point in the log is marked one or the other. This keeps people from rewriting everything because of one complaint.

Fix in this order

  • First: every point that appears in two or more logs
  • Then: every blocking point
  • Then run a second round

Module 4 · 5 / 8

Instructor notes. One log can be noise from that request or that session. The same point twice means the instructions are wrong.

The author is the worst reader of their own instructions

A typical find: one step says stop if the answer is yes, while every other step says go on if yes. The author reads past it every time. A stranger trips on it at once.

Module 4 · 6 / 8

Instructor notes. This is the best argument for the test. Ask: what would a stranger trip on in your instructions? Then stop guessing and run the test.

Lab

Module 4 · 7 / 8

Instructor notes. Twenty minutes. Make sure every new chat really is new. Memory or custom instructions can carry the first session over and make the test pass for the wrong reason.

Take away

  • A stranger tests it; you do not help

1. A fresh chat finishes the job and lists twelve problems. Did the test pass?

Show the answer

Yes: it finished, and the twelve problems are the next hour of work. The list is the test working.

Show the answer

A different guess would change the result. Minor points would not change the result.

Show the answer

Otherwise it carries what you said earlier and passes for the wrong reason. A stranger has only your instructions.

What to expect

Module 2: Decide: is it worth building? · Lesson 2.2

Reuse check

About 15 minutes · Step 2 of nine

You will: Find out whether something you already have, or something free, does the job. The cheapest tool is the one you do not build.

Do this

  1. Read what this step is for (above) and what to expect (below).
  2. Open the chat you used in lesson 1.3, or a new one, and paste the prompt. Fill in the brackets.
  3. Check what comes back against "Proof you are done". Tick each one you have.
  4. Answer the gate question. If the answer is no, fix the step before you go on.
  5. Add the result to your plan.

What to expect

About 10 minutes with web search, longer without. Expect to find something that does part of the job. Often the result is that you build less than you planned.

Who it helps

  • Beginner As someone with a job I repeat every week, I want to know whether my chatbot already does this, so that I do not spend a week rebuilding a built-in feature.
  • Advanced As a builder working with a coding agent, I want links and dates for what exists, so that I can wrap an existing tool and not start from nothing.
  • Expert As a team lead whose agents work unattended, I want the reuse check written down, so that nobody on the team builds what we already pay for.

Prompt to paste

Before I build anything, check what already exists for this job: [the one-sentence job from step 1].

Look in this order: features already built into the assistant I use ([name it]), free and open-source tools, and how people who do this well usually do it. Search the web if you can. If you cannot, say so and list what I should look up.

Give me a table: the option, what it covers, what it costs, its limits, a link, and today's date. Do not invent a product or a price.

Then tell me plainly: does something existing do the whole job? If yes, tell me to stop, and write me a short guide to it. If no, say in one line what is left to build.

Proof you are done

The gate for step 2. After what already exists, is there still something to build? If not, stop and write a guide to that thing.

Module 2: Decide: is it worth building? · Lesson 2.3

Outside help and cost

About 10 minutes · Steps 3 and 4 of nine

You will: For whatever is left, list the services you could pay for, so that the choice between building and buying is made on purpose. Put a price and a limit on everything the first version depends on. A tool people cannot afford to run is not finished.

Do this

  1. Read what this step is for (above) and what to expect (below).
  2. Open the chat you used in lesson 1.3, or a new one, and paste the prompts. Fill in the brackets.
  3. Check what comes back against "Proof you are done". Tick each one you have.
  4. Answer the gate question. If the answer is no, fix the step before you go on.
  5. Add the result to your plan.

What to expect

About 5 minutes. For most beginner tools the answer is that there is nothing to buy. Write that down and go on. About 5 minutes. Expect the first version to cost nothing new. If it needs something paid, that usually belongs in a later level.

Who it helps

  • Beginner As someone with a job I repeat every week, I want to know what I would have to pay for, so that there are no surprises after I start.
  • Advanced As a builder working with a coding agent, I want the options listed and not ranked, so that I choose with the cost and the way out in view.
  • Expert As a team lead whose agents work unattended, I want build-or-buy decided for the first version only, so that we do not sign up for a service before we know the tool is used.

Prompt to paste

Here is what is left to build after the reuse check: [paste the one line from step 2].

List any paid services that could do part of it. For each: what it does, the price, the free limit if there is one, and what happens to me if it closes or raises its price. List them evenly. Do not choose one for me, and do not invent a price: write "not confirmed" if you cannot check it.

Then, for each part of the tool, say whether the first version should build it, buy it, or leave it as something I do by hand.

Prompt to paste

List everything the first version of this tool depends on: [describe the first version in a sentence, and name the assistant and the plan you use].

For each dependency give: what it is for, its price, its usage limit, and who pays (me, or each person who uses the tool). Write "not confirmed" where you cannot check a price or a limit.

End with one line: does the first version cost nothing new, or how much? If it needs anything paid, suggest how to do without it in the first version.

Proof you are done

The gate for step 3. Do you know what you would build and what you would buy?

The gate for step 4. Does the first version cost nothing, or an amount you accept?

Then draw it as a short list a beginner could copy: what goes in, what the AI does, what comes out, who checks it. Use the fewest parts that work. If you add a part, say what breaks without it.

This layout is now fixed. Tell me if anything later would force it to change.

Module 3: Design: the plan before the build · Lesson 3.2

Plan and front door

About 15 minutes · Step 6 of nine

You will: Write the one-page plan and the first thing a user will see, before the tool exists. If the front door cannot be written, the tool is not understood yet.

Do this

  1. Read what this step is for (above) and what to expect (below).
  2. Open the chat you used in lesson 1.3, or a new one, and paste the prompt. Fill in the brackets.
  3. Check what comes back against "Proof you are done". Tick each one you have.
  4. Answer the gate question. If the answer is no, fix the step before you go on.
  5. Add the result to your plan.

What to expect

About 10 minutes. Expect to cut. If a section of the plan needs more than five lines, the scope has grown.

Who it helps

  • Beginner As someone with a job I repeat every week, I want one page I can read in two minutes, so that I know what I am about to build and how I will test it.
  • Advanced As a builder working with a coding agent, I want the first screen of the README written first, so that the agent builds toward what a user will actually try.
  • Expert As a team lead whose agents work unattended, I want one worked example for each kind of request, so that a reviewer can compare a real result against a known good one.

Prompt to paste

Write the one-page plan for this tool from what we have decided so far. Use these headings: the job, the user, what is out, the wow, what already exists, build or buy, cost, the design, the folder layout, the steps, the front door, the test, who reviewed it, gates, next three additions. Five lines at most under each. Mark anything you had to assume.

Then write the front door, the first thing a new user reads: one sentence that would make a stranger stop, two sentences on who it is for and what they get, and one thing they can try in five minutes.

Then write one worked example for each kind of request the tool handles differently (most first versions need one): a realistic input and the result I should expect. Say that each example is invented.

Proof you are done

The gate for step 6. Is the beginner version fully described?

Module 4: Build and test: let a stranger break it · Lesson 4.3

Read the friction log and fix

About 15 minutes

You will: Turn a friction log into a short fix list in the right order.

Do this

  1. Read the friction log before the result.
  2. Mark each point BLOCKING (a different guess would change the result) or MINOR.
  3. Fix first every point that appears in two or more logs, then every blocking point.
  4. Run the test again in another new chat.
  5. Write one line: did round 2 find fewer blocking points?

Grow

About 10 minutes · Step 9 of nine

You will: Add one thing at a time, and only after the level below is in use. Small additions get reviewed. Big ones get waved through.

Do this

What to expect

No fixed time: this step repeats. Expect most of your first list of additions to turn out unnecessary once you use the tool.

Who it helps
