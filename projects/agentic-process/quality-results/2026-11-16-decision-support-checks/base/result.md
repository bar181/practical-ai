# Should Harbour Lane Bakery start weekday office catering?

## Short answer

**Yes, but as a small, capped 90-day pilot, not a full catering launch.** Start with a short "office box" menu made only from items you already bake (pastry and bread boxes, cookie trays, coffee for groups), order-ahead only, Tuesday to Thursday, delivered within a short radius of your three shops. Keep the head baker's hours off-limits, spend well under half of your $25,000, and decide at day 90 using numbers agreed with your partner up front.

Why not a full catering service now: your biggest constraint is not money or demand, it's people. A full lunch catering menu (sandwiches, salads, hot food) adds new production, cold-chain handling and early-morning pressure on a team you've said you don't want to burn out, with a head baker who already works six days. A narrow pilot tests the demand without betting the team on it.

---

## How this fits your three goals

| Goal | How a pilot helps | Honest limit |
|---|---|---|
| Grow sales 10% (about $140,000) | Adds a new revenue stream with larger tickets than walk-in sales | Unlikely to cover the full 10% alone in year one. Treat it as one contributor alongside in-shop moves |
| Raise weekday afternoon sales | Use quiet afternoon hours to pack next-day orders, and sell a "3 pm meeting box" (coffee plus treats) delivered in the afternoon | Most office catering is morning and lunch, so the afternoon gain is mostly *labour use* and a smaller afternoon-delivery product |
| Keep the team from burning out | Caps, lead times and a menu limited to existing products keep it controllable | Without those guardrails, catering is a classic burnout source. The guardrails are not optional |

## What the research says

**Demand is real and growing, mostly because employers are using food to get people back to the office.**
- ezCater's latest survey says [91% of workplaces plan to spend the same or more on food in 2026, up from 82% in 2024, and daily or weekly employee meal programs grew 26% year over year](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260505698331/en/). Employers cover the cost for 81% of those meals. This is US-heavy survey data, so treat it as direction, not a Hamilton forecast.
- An earlier ezCater report found [39% of businesses that order catering now order weekly, up from 32% the year before](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/ezcater-report-74-percent-operators-expect-20-percent-catering-growth/715406/). Recurring weekly orders are what make this worthwhile for you.
- Return to office is picking up in Canada. [Nearly half of Ontario's roughly 60,000 public service workers were set to return to the office full time](https://www.ctvnews.ca/toronto/article/tens-of-thousands-of-ontario-public-servants-return-to-office-full-time-next-week-heres-what-that-could-mean-for-toronto/) at the start of 2026, and [Colliers reports national office vacancy fell to 13.4% in Q2 2026, the fourth straight quarterly decline](https://www.cp24.com/news/money/2026/07/09/office-industrial-real-estate-vacancies-dropping-amid-strong-demand-colliers-report/).
- When Ottawa's federal workers moved to three office days, one downtown coffee shop owner [reported business up 30 to 40 per cent on the first three days of the week](https://amp.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.7350235). That's one shop, but it shows that office demand clusters midweek, which is why the pilot runs Tuesday to Thursday.
- A Toronto startup, Nüu Catering, is building a business on exactly this, targeting [offices with 20 to 200 employees, especially for recurring orders](https://retail-insider.com/retail-insider/2026/06/toronto-startup-nuu-catering-bets-office-catering-can-help-drive-return-to-office-culture/), and notes that office orders are placed in advance and outside peak hours, which gives more predictable daytime sales. That's a potential channel partner later, and a sign of competition coming.

**What I could not find:** reliable Hamilton-specific office vacancy or office-attendance data. Your local demand is the biggest unknown, which is why step 1 below is a cheap demand test before you spend.

**Bakeries do this successfully, but it stays a modest share of sales.** An older industry survey (2017, US) found [specialty bakeries earn about 10% of sales from catering](https://bakemag.com/articles/5589-specialty-bakeries-the-catering-boom). The same piece warns that clients increasingly expect more than basic cookies and brownies, and that successful bakeries keep a focused selection rather than becoming a one-stop caterer.

**What office buyers care about:** the person ordering is managing risk. They want to know [everyone will like the food, it will arrive on time, and it will reflect well on them](https://donutdigest.beehiiv.com/p/the-catering-opportunity-most-bakeries-miss). Free samples, clear pricing and simple ordering are the levers. ezCater also found that [detailed menus with photos, dietary options, drinks and desserts, and both individual and tray packaging get twice as many bookings](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260505698331/en/).

**Operations is where catering fails.** ChowNow's guide puts it bluntly: ["Most catering failures are operational, not culinary."](https://get.chownow.com/blog/restaurant-catering-guide-for-a-high-revenue-channel/) It recommends 8 to 12 items that travel well, order minimums, 24 to 48 hour lead times, and a named person who packs and hands off each order.

**Selling through marketplaces costs a lot.** ChowNow cites [ezCater's commission at 15%](https://get.chownow.com/blog/restaurant-catering-guide-for-a-high-revenue-channel/) (that's a competitor's figure, so confirm directly). General delivery apps in Canada are higher: [Uber Eats and DoorDash plans run 15% to 30%, with effective rates estimated above 30%](https://www.dineopen.com/blog/food-delivery-commission-comparison-canada-2026). Take orders directly for the pilot.

**Food safety rules apply in transit.** Under Ontario's Food Premises regulation, potentially hazardous food must be kept at [4°C or lower, or 60°C or higher, including while transported](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/170493). This is a big reason to start with pastries, bread and baked goods rather than meat, egg or dairy-filled sandwiches and salads, which need cold-chain equipment and checks.

## The pilot I'd recommend

**Scope**
- **Menu (6 to 10 items, all already on your daily production):** morning pastry box, bread and spreads board, cookie and square tray, "3 pm meeting box," coffee traveller (serves about 10), add-ons like juice or fruit. Prices per person shown clearly, with small and large sizes.
- **Days and timing:** Tuesday to Thursday, order by 2 pm two business days ahead. Morning deliveries plus a 2 to 3:30 pm afternoon slot.
- **Area:** a short delivery radius around each shop (you pick, for example 10 minutes' drive).
- **Daily cap:** for example, no more than 3 orders per shop per day at first. Say "sold out" rather than overload the team.
- **Minimum order:** for example, 10 people or $150. Set it so every delivery is worth the driver's time.
- **Ordering:** a simple web form or email plus invoice. No marketplace yet.

**Team guardrails (the most important part)**
- The head baker's hours do not go up. Extra volume is baked by other staff or slotted into existing bakes.
- Afternoon shop staff pack next-day orders during the quiet window. That's how you turn slow afternoons into productive hours.
- One named "catering lead" (a keen existing staff member, with a small raise or bonus) owns the order calendar, confirmations and delivery schedule.
- Track hours. If overtime or head-baker hours rise, the pilot pauses.

**Budget (well under the $25,000 limit)**

These are planning estimates, not quotes. Get prices locally.

| Item | Rough budget |
|---|---|
| Insulated carriers, coffee dispensers, delivery crates | $2,000 to $3,500 |
| Branded boxes, labels, serving supplies (first batches) | $1,500 to $2,500 |
| Simple online order form and invoicing setup | $500 to $1,500 |
| Free sample boxes for 30 to 40 local workplaces | $1,500 to $2,500 |
| Extra staff hours for packing and delivery during the ramp-up | $3,000 to $5,000 |
| **Total** | **about $8,500 to $15,000** |

That leaves at least $10,000 in reserve for whichever growth idea wins, catering or something else.

**Rough economics to test (assumptions, not data)**

If an average order is about 12 people at about $15 a head ($180), and you reach 3 orders a week per shop, that's roughly $1,600 a week, or about $75,000 to $85,000 a year once running, around 5 to 6% of current sales. A slower ramp in year one is more realistic. Delivery labour (a staff member's time and mileage, perhaps $20 to $30 per order) is the cost that most often eats the margin, so measure it from day one.

## Steps and decision points

1. **Weeks 1 to 2: demand test (under $3,000).** Make a list of workplaces near each shop: offices, clinics, real estate and law firms, schools, community agencies. Drop off sample boxes with a menu card. **Go to step 2 only if** at least 8 to 10 workplaces book a paid first order or say they'd order regularly.
2. **Weeks 3 to 4: set up.** Buy carriers and packaging, set up the order form, train the catering lead, and run two or three practice orders for friends or regulars.
3. **Weeks 5 to 16: run the pilot.** Track the scorecard weekly.
4. **Day 90: go, adjust or stop**, using thresholds you and your partner agree on now.

**Suggested day-90 scorecard (agree the targets with your partner before you start)**

| Measure | Example "go" threshold |
|---|---|
| Orders per week (all shops) | 8 or more and rising |
| Repeat customers | At least a third of customers have ordered twice |
| Profit per order after packaging and delivery labour | At least as good as your in-shop margin |
| On-time and complete deliveries | 95% or better |
| Head baker hours and team overtime | No increase |
| Staff feedback | No rise in complaints or sick days |

If it passes: add a second delivery slot, consider simple lunch items (with proper cold-chain equipment and food safety checks), and look at recurring weekly contracts or a partner platform. If it's borderline: keep it as a small order-ahead service with no delivery (pickup only). If it fails: stop, and you've spent under $15,000 to learn it.

---

## Risks to discuss with your partner

- **Burnout and quality creep.** The real risk. Caps and the "existing items only" rule are your protection.
- **Unknown local demand.** No Hamilton-specific data found. The sample-box test addresses this cheaply.
- **Delivery cost and reliability.** One late order to an office manager can lose the account. Start with a radius you can reliably serve.
- **Revenue gap.** Catering alone probably won't deliver the full 10% growth in year one. Pair it with in-shop moves for weekday afternoons (for example an afternoon coffee and pastry offer, or a bread pre-order and pickup).
- **Food safety.** Stay with shelf-stable baked goods until you have cold-chain equipment and processes for anything perishable.

---

## One-paragraph version for your partner

> We should test weekday office catering with a 90-day pilot, not a full launch. Demand for workplace food is growing as offices bring people back, and office orders are larger and more predictable than walk-in sales. But our real constraint is the team, so the pilot uses only items we already make, runs Tuesday to Thursday with order caps and two-day lead times, doesn't add hours for our head baker, and uses quiet afternoon staff time to pack orders. It costs roughly $8,500 to $15,000 of our $25,000, starts with a two-week sample-box test with local workplaces, and we decide at day 90 against targets we agree on now: orders per week, repeat customers, profit per order after delivery, and no increase in overtime.
