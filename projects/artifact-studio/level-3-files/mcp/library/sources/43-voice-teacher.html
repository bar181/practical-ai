Today's question

How do you answer everyone within the hour, without hiring anyone?

Think of it as

The host at a busy restaurant.

The host does not cook your meal. The host looks up, says "we see you, your table is nearly ready", and tells the kitchen you are here. You stop wondering whether to leave.

A first reply does the same job. It is not the quote. It is the looking up.

A real example

One enquiry, minute by minute

  1. 6:40A message arrives through the website form.
  2. 6:41A draft reply is ready. It thanks her by name, repeats what she asked for and offers two visit dates.
  3. 6:55The office manager reads it, changes one date and presses send.
  4. 6:56The customer has an answer. Sixteen minutes, start to finish.
In pseudocode

First, the simple version

when an enquiry arrives:  if the office is closed:    send "got it, reply by 9"  draft a reply from our template  if the draft contains a guess:    mark the guess  show the draft to a person  if the person approves:    send it

Check yourself

Three questions before you go

Why does a person still press send?

Because a draft can be wrong, and a wrong promise costs more than a slow one. The machine does the typing. The person does the deciding.

What should the first reply never include?

A price. The host does not quote the bill at the door. The first reply confirms, sets a time and stops.

What happens at 2 in the morning?

Lines 2 and 3. An instant note says a person will reply by 9. The draft waits for that person.

So

The first reply is the looking up, not the meal. Draft it by machine. Send it by hand.

Behind this page

The teacher voice makes a hard idea feel obvious by starting somewhere familiar. Inspired by David Malan's lectures. The company and the timings are invented for the demo.

How it is built

  1. One question, stated plainly
  2. An everyday analogy in very large type
  3. One concrete example with real times
  4. The same idea as pseudocode, stepped through line by line
  5. A harder case, handled by lines already on the board
  6. Questions to check understanding
  7. A one-sentence recap that reuses the analogy

Design rules

  • Black and white, with one highlight colour that means "look here"
  • The analogy gets the biggest type on the page
  • Complexity rises one step at a time, never two
  • Code is set in a monospace face with numbered lines
  • Every section is tagged with what kind of thing it is
  • The reader does something: steps through, then answers
