Analogy

Think of an assistant who has a reply typed and waiting on your desk before you sit down. You read it, change what is wrong and sign it. Nothing leaves without your signature.

The typing is the draft. The signature is the person who approves every reply.

Steps: five, about an hour in all

This is how we set it up. Tick each step when it is done.

  1. 1Collect three replies you are proud of

    Who
    You.
    Do
    Find three replies to enquiries that you have already sent and are proud of.
    Fact
    We collected three. All five steps took us about an hour.

  2. 2Write your rules on half a page

    Who
    You.
    Do
    Write down what a first reply must do and what it must never do.
    Fact
    Ours fit on half a page:
    • A person approves every reply.
    • The first reply confirms and offers a visit.
    • It never states a price.

  3. 3Save both in a chat project

    Who
    You.
    Do
    Save the three replies and the half page of rules together in one chat project.
    Fact
    The whole thing costs us about $150 a month.

  4. 4Test it on five old enquiries

    Who
    You.
    Do
    Run five old enquiries through it before you use it on a real one.
    Fact
    Our enquiries arrive by web form, email and telephone.

  5. 5Use it on the next real enquiry

    Who
    A member of staff, every time.
    Do
    A reply is drafted the moment the message arrives. A member of staff reads it, fixes it and sends it. [needs: how a new message reaches the chat project so that a reply is drafted at once]
    Fact
    Our trial covered all 214 enquiries over 90 days.

Mistakes to avoid

Each one is a rule from our half page, turned round.

MistakeOur rule
Sending a draft that nobody has read.A person approves every reply.
Stating a price in the first reply.The first reply never states a price.
Replying without offering a visit.The first reply confirms and offers a visit.
