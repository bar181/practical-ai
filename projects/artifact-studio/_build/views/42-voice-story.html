The hook

At 6:40 on a Tuesday evening, walking the dog along the water, Priya finally sent the message she had been putting off for a year.

A character who wants something

She wanted a kitchen her family could actually eat in. Not a showroom. A table that fit four people and a counter where the kettle did not have to be moved to make toast.

It had taken her a year to ask. Asking meant admitting the old kitchen had won.

She typed four sentences, pressed send, and put the phone back in her pocket.

The problem

Then nothing happened.

  • On the surfaceThree companies had her message. By Wednesday night, none had answered.
  • UnderneathShe began to feel foolish for asking. Maybe the job was too small. Maybe she had filled in the form wrong.
  • Why it is wrongNobody should have to chase a company to give it their money.
A guide appears

We know, because we were the ones who went quiet.

For years our own replies took a day or more. Not because we did not care. Because the person who could answer was on a ladder in someone else's kitchen.

So we changed one thing. Now a reply is drafted the moment a message arrives, and a person reads it, fixes it and sends it. In a 90-day trial, 81 of every 100 people heard from us within the hour.

The plan

Three steps, and only the first is yours.

  1. You write to us once. Four sentences is plenty.
  2. A real person answers within the hour, in working hours.
  3. That first reply offers you two dates for a visit.
The call to action

What is at stake
If nothing changesAnother year of moving the kettle. Another form sent into silence. The quiet conclusion that it was never going to happen.
If you write tonightAn answer before bed. A date in the diary. Someone in your kitchen this week with a tape measure and a pencil.
The ending

Priya's phone buzzed before she reached the end of the path. On Thursday someone stood in her kitchen with a tape measure.

The reply was not the work. It was the proof that the work would be done.

Behind this page

The storyteller voice makes the customer the hero and the company the guide. Inspired by Donald Miller's story framework. Priya, the company and the figures are invented for the demo.

How it is built

  1. A hook: one moment, one person, a time on the clock
  2. A character who wants something specific
  3. A problem at three depths: on the surface, underneath, and why it is wrong
  4. A guide who shows empathy first, then proof
  5. A plan in three steps
  6. One call to action
  7. The stakes: failure beside success
  8. An ending that returns to the opening, and a line that lingers

Design rules

  • Photography leads. Slow push-ins and a window that drifts, like a nature film.
  • One reading column, large serif type, generous space
  • The story beat is named above each part, small, in the accent colour
  • One pull quote per screen at most
  • The company does not appear until the fourth beat
  • Colours come from the photograph: dusk, sand and ember
