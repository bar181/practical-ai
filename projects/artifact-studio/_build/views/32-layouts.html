Layouts

A layout decides where the eye goes first and what it meets next. Six cover most pages. Pick by what the reader has to do, not by what looks new.

The reader has toUse
Read something throughSingle column
Scan a long page for the part they needHeadings down the left
Do one thingCorner to corner
Weigh two things, or see a picture beside a messageSplit screen
Browse many things of one kindCard grid
Take in several different things at a glanceBento grid

Single column

One column of text at a comfortable width, with nothing beside it. The layout for anything meant to be read from top to bottom.

  • Use it for reports, explainers, letters, anything over 300 words.
  • The rule keep lines to about 60 to 75 characters. Wider than that and readers lose their place.
  • Avoid it when the reader is comparing things. A column hides one option while showing another.

Headings down the left

On a long page people read the first lines across, then run down the left edge looking for a heading that matters to them. This is often called the F-pattern. Give the left edge something worth finding.

  • Use it for guides, questions and answers, policies, search results.
  • The rule start every heading and every first sentence with the words that carry the meaning. "Refunds: 14 days" beats "Information about our approach to refunds".
  • Avoid it when the page has one job. Then the reader should not have to hunt.

Corner to corner

On a page with few words the eye starts top left, crosses to the top right, drops diagonally and ends bottom right. This is the Z-pattern. Put your name where the eye starts and the button where it ends.

  • Use it for landing pages, sign-up pages, a social picture, the first screen of a proposal.
  • The rule one action, placed where the eye finishes. A long page repeats the shape: picture left then right, section by section.
  • Avoid it when there is a lot to read. The eye will not zigzag through paragraphs.

Split screen

Two halves of equal weight: a message and a picture, or two choices. On a phone the halves stack, so decide which comes first.

  • Use it for a message that needs its picture, a before and after, a choice between two paths.
  • The rule the halves must be equals. If one is clearly less important, it is a sidebar, and a sidebar should be narrower.
  • Avoid it when there are three things. Three halves do not exist.

Card grid

Rows of matching cards. Nothing is more important than anything else, which is the point: the reader browses and chooses.

  • Use it for products, services, articles, a team, anything where the items are of one kind.
  • The rule every card the same height, every picture the same shape, the same order inside each.
  • Avoid it when one item matters most. A grid cannot say so.

Bento grid

Tiles of different sizes packed into one block, like a lunch box. One tile is clearly the largest. The rest support it.

  • Use it for a summary of several different things: a result, a quote, a number, a picture.
  • The rule one dominant tile. Keep the same padding and the same corner in every tile, or it looks like a jumble.
  • Avoid it when the items are of one kind. Then it is a card grid pretending.
