# Weekly brief: landscape review

What established practice says about a short recurring brief. Checked on 2026-11-15, before
anything further is built. This replaces the earlier notes.

## Summary

- **Put the bottom line first.** Every source found gives the same advice: the main point,
  decision or request opens the brief, and the support follows. The simple level opens with
  "the week in three lines", which matches.
- **A brief exists to drive a decision.** Sources say to state the ask and its date at the top.
  The skill did not require this. It does now.
- **Weekly is the usual rhythm.** Daily is too often to be read and quarterly is too late.
- **Nothing new needs building for the simple level.** A topic list, a chat assistant that can
  search, and one page of instructions.

## Established practice

| Practice | What the sources say | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bottom line up front | The main point, decision or request comes in the opening sentence, then the reasons. The practice comes from military writing | [Science of People](https://www.scienceofpeople.com/bottom-line-up-front/), [Board Intelligence](https://www.boardintelligence.com/blog/great-communication-call-your-bluf) |
| Put the question for the reader first | A reader who meets the question at the start reads the rest with it in mind | [Board Intelligence](https://www.boardintelligence.com/blog/great-communication-call-your-bluf) |
| State the ask and its date | The opening names the status, what is needed and by when | [project-management.com](https://project-management.com/your-stakeholders-are-not-reading-status-reports/) |
| Recommend, do not only inform | The best weekly reports say what should happen next, with an owner and a date | [Teamwork](https://www.teamwork.com/blog/weekly-project-report/) |
| "Nothing needs you" is a valid bottom line | A brief can open by saying things are on track and no action is needed | [Science of People](https://www.scienceofpeople.com/bottom-line-up-front/) |
| Weekly rhythm | Weekly reports are among the most common kind, and give a balanced view without too much detail | [Asana](https://asana.com/resources/how-project-status-reports), [Weekdone](https://blog.weekdone.com/?p=1701) |
| Ask the reader what they need | Fit the brief to how the reader works before assuming the default format is right | [project-management.com](https://project-management.com/your-stakeholders-are-not-reading-status-reports/) |

These are vendor and trade guides, not studies. They agree with each other, which makes this
agreed practice. It is not a measured finding.

## How this tool follows it

| Practice | In this tool |
| --- | --- |
| Bottom line up front | The brief opens with "the week in three lines" |
| State the ask and its date | **Added after this review.** If the brief needs a decision from the reader, one of the three lines says so, with the date it is needed by |
| "Nothing needs you" | **Added after this review.** If nothing needs a decision, the brief says so in the three lines |
| Recommend, do not only inform | Not followed. The brief reports what was decided and what was left open. It does not recommend. That is deliberate for now and worth testing with readers |
| A source on every claim | Every outside item carries a link and a date. Inside items name the document they came from |
