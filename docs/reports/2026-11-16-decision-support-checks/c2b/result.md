# The brief

**Decision:** Should Harbour Lane Bakery start a weekday catering service for offices near its shops?

**Recommendation:** Do not launch a full catering service yet; instead run an 8-week, capped catering pilot from one shop, using only items you already bake, and before you start, check demand by talking to 10 office managers near that shop.

**Why:**
- I could not find any data on how many office workers are near your shops or how often they order, and the one Hamilton market report I found says downtown office space "has continued to struggle." Demand is the fact that decides this, and it is not found.
- A full service adds early production and deliveries to a team you want to protect, with a head baker already at six days. A pilot built from existing products adds the least load.
- Catering alone is unlikely to hit your 10 percent goal: that needs about $140,000 more a year, roughly 18 orders of $165 every week (estimate). A pilot tells you cheaply whether that is realistic.

**SWOT (for the pilot):**

|---|---|

| Room to price under the Hamilton Convention Centre, which charges $19.95 a person for a pastry-and-coffee breakfast plus a 17% facility fee (source) | No delivery setup or ordering process yet (assumed) |
| Spend capped at $3,000 (proposal) | 8 weeks is a small sample (proposal) |
| **Opportunities** | **Threats** |

| A 2 to 4 pm "meeting tray" could add afternoon sales (proposal) | Food safety rules apply while food is transported (source) |

**Risks and how to reduce them:**
1. *Staff stretch.* Cap the pilot at 10 orders a week, prep orders in the afternoon lull, and add no hours for the head baker.
2. *Thin margins.* Price each item from its real cost plus delivery time; minimum order of 10 people.
3. *Food safety and insurance.* Keep the pilot to items that do not need refrigeration; ask Hamilton Public Health and your insurance broker before the first delivery.

**Next steps (next 30 days):**
1. Agree the pilot rules with your partner: one shop, 8 weeks, $3,000 cap, and the success test below.
2. List the offices within a 10-minute walk of each shop, then talk to 10 office managers: what they order now, how often, and what they pay.
3. Call Hamilton Public Health, your insurance broker and your accountant (HST on catering orders), then write a four-item menu.

**What would change this recommendation:** strong demand in your talks (5 or more of 10 offices saying they would order monthly) would justify a bigger pilot; no offices near any shop, or a public health requirement for kitchen changes, would mean doing nothing and looking for another way to lift afternoon sales.

**Assumptions:** your kitchens have spare capacity in the afternoon; other staff can pack and deliver; the $25,000 covers setup and first-year losses, not ongoing wages.

# How I got there

## Step 1. Frame the decision

Should Harbour Lane Bakery add a weekday catering service for offices near its three shops?

Options:
- **A. Do nothing.** Keep selling to walk-in customers only.
- **B. Full weekday catering service.** Breakfast and lunch menus from all three shops, delivery, an ordering process and marketing to nearby offices.
- **C. Small pilot (proposal).** One shop, 8 weeks, morning pastry-and-coffee boxes plus a 2 to 4 pm meeting tray (cookies, squares, coffee), built only from items you already bake. Order by 2 pm the day before, minimum 10 people, delivery within about 2 km or pickup, capped at 10 orders a week, spend capped at $3,000. This tests the riskiest part, demand, cheaply.

## Step 3. Compare and recommend

**Limits (pass or fail):**

**Scores against goals (vs doing nothing, next twelve months):**

| Goal | B. Full service | C. Pilot |
|---|---|---|
| Grow sales 10% | 0. Could add sales, but it rests on local office demand, which is not found (judgement call) | 0. Adds at most about 2% even if kept up (estimate), and demand is not found |
| Raise weekday afternoon sales | 0. Office catering leans to morning and lunch; afternoon demand not found (judgement call) | 0. The 2 to 4 pm tray is aimed at this, but demand is not found |
| Keep the team from burning out | -1. Earlier starts, more production and deliveries for the same team | 0. Capped orders, existing items, prep in the afternoon lull |
| **Total** | **-1** | **0** |

**Leading option:** Nothing scores above 0, so doing nothing leads. But a not-found fact (office demand near your shops) could change that, so I recommend the pilot, option C, as the check.

**Check first:** Because demand is not found, the cheapest step that settles it comes before even the pilot: 10 conversations with office managers near the chosen shop. Then the pilot itself is the paid trial. Proposed success test for the pilot: at least 4 paid orders a week by week 8, at least 2 repeat customers, a margin no worse than your shop average, and no extra hours for the head baker.

**Judgement calls:**
- *The $25,000.* I read it as setup costs plus any first-year losses. The other reading counts ongoing wages too, which would make the full service more likely to fail the limit. It would not change the leading option.
- *Afternoon sales.* I read catering as mostly morning and lunch, so I gave it no credit on this goal. If you count prep work done in the afternoon as using afternoon capacity, that helps costs, not sales. It would not change the leading option.
- *Sales scores.* I scored the full service 0 on sales because demand is not found. If your talks show strong demand, it could score +1, but with -1 on burnout its total would be 0 and still not lead.
- *Longer view.* Over two to three years, steady office accounts could become a reliable weekday channel worth a second baker. That is a reason to run the pilot well, not to skip it.
